## Job Market Paper

**GenAI Governance in School: How Guidelines and Enforecement Shape Student Performance** 

(with Jinghao Jia, Xu Zhang; slides available upon request)

**Abstract**
We investigate the governance of Generative AI in education. Using a randomized controlled trial in two urban middle schools in China, we evaluate two interventions: instructional AI Guidelines provision and Enforcement (defined as a 30% cap on AI-generated content). We find that neither component is sufficient in isolation. Guidelines alone induce moral hazard: male students increase AI over-reliance relative to an unregulated control. Enforcement alone fares no better. Only the bundle generates robust writing gains of 5.1 percentage points ($0.20\sigma$). The violation rate patterns across arms reveal why: for boys, guidelines without enforcement produce the highest non-compliance, reversed only when enforcement penalizes over-reliance; for girls, guidelines alone trigger a sustained decline in violations, consistent with norm internalization. These gender-asymmetric compliance patterns imply that effective governance must solve both a preference-formation and a commitment problem, but the binding constraint differs by gender. Guidelines-only policies may harm the very students they intend to help.           

## Working Papers

**Closer is not equal: The proximity penalty in community-based mental health expansion in China** (Under review) 
(with Yuzhi Hao, Xu Zhang) 

**Abstract**
Low- and middle-income countries have converged on a two-pronged policy design to close mental-health treatment gaps: pair community-based supply expansion with public stigma-reduction campaigns. The design assumes that physical accessibility and social acceptability are separable dimensions of access --- a premise embedded in canonical access frameworks. We argue this separability premise breaks down when stigmatized services are delivered through community-embedded channels, and identify the resulting proximity penalty: physical proximity simultaneously reduces travel cost and amplifies the marginal disutility of stigma, generating severity-graded rather than uniform welfare gains. We specify three scope conditions under which the penalty activates. We test the argument on China's nationwide Social Psychological Service System Construction Pilot (2018--2021) using difference-in-differences on the China Family Panel Studies. The Plan improves population mental health on average --- a 0.055 standard deviation reduction in standardized distress scales and a 16 percent relative reduction in clinical caseness --- but gains concentrate among low-risk individuals; the high-risk group shows no detectable improvement. A discrete choice experiment provides preference-level evidence consistent with the mechanism: high-risk respondents are more price-sensitive but less responsive to commuting-time reductions, and a three-way interaction shows that proximity differentially amplifies the deterrent effect of stigma for them. We translate the mechanism into severity-contingent delivery-design guidance.

**Benefit Salience and Cognitive Barriers to Programme Take-up: A Field Experiment** (Under review) 
(with Xu Zhang)

**Abstract**
Low take-up of welfare-improving programmes is often attributed to liquidity constraints, yet participation may lag even when monetary costs are negligible. We isolate the role of cognitive barriers and trust frictions in the context of China’s Rural Toilet Revolution, a unique setting where full government funding effectively rules out the inability to pay channel. To disentangle the content of the signal from its source, we implemented a $3 \times 2$ factorial RCT that cross-randomises three information frames (benefits, harms, or technical details) against the presence of a peer messenger. We document a sharp divergence between stated intentions and revealed preferences. While all interventions uniformly increased short-run self-reported willingness to participate, actual administrative enrolment responded significantly only to benefit-salience messages. We detect no effect of peer messengers. Heterogeneity analyses show that enrolment effects are concentrated among villagers with higher baseline programme literacy, suggesting a complementarity between information and prior knowledge. These results imply that even when financial constraints are removed, the inability to quantify invisible returns remains a binding constraint, and effective policy must resolve this valuation gap to trigger adoption.

**Risk Tolerance, Soft Skills, and Sectoral Sorting: Lab-in-the-field Experiments in Urban India's Dual Informal Labor Market** (Under review)

(with Raja Rajendra Timilsina, Dil B Rahut, Tetsushi Sonobe)

**Abstract** 
The persistence of informal employment in developing economies raises central economic questions: why do some workers engage with digital platforms while others remain in traditional informal markets? What are the underlying economic preferences, despite the rapid expansion of the informal gig economy? This paper addresses this puzzle by using a lab-in-the-field experiment in urban India. We develop a framework in which workers are ex-ante sorted into digital or non-digital sectors. We then conduct experiments to collect data on risk tolerance, soft skills, and working conditions, while holding mobility to be limited. Our results reveal that younger non-digital workers exhibit higher risk tolerance than digital participants, while older digital workers tend to be more risk-neutral or risk-averse. Moreover, digital workers exhibit greater self-control and openness, but lower grit and conscientiousness, relative to their traditional-sector counterparts. These findings suggest that occupational sorting in secondary labor markets reflects heterogeneous preferences over income risk, flexibility, and community-based security, rather than simple technological adoption. This sorting mechanism in a utility-maximization model with entry frictions can be formalized to a broader understanding of labor market segmentation and the dynamics of informality in developing economies. The results have implications for policies aimed at improving welfare in dual labor markets, including the design of risk-sharing mechanisms, skill-building initiatives, and regulatory frameworks for digital platforms to scale up in the future.
