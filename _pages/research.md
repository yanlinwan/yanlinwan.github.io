## Working Papers
**GenAI Governance in School: How Guidelines and Enforecement Shape Student Performance** (with Jinghao Jia, Xu Zhang) Under Review

**Abstract**
We investigate the governance of Generative AI in education. Using a randomized controlled trial in two urban middle schools in China, we evaluate two interventions: instructional AI Guidelines provision and Enforcement (defined as a 30% cap on AI-generated content). We find that neither component is sufficient in isolation. Guidelines alone induce moral hazard: male students increase AI over-reliance relative to an unregulated control. Enforcement alone fares no better. Only the bundle generates robust writing gains of 5.1 percentage points ($0.20\sigma$). The violation rate patterns across arms reveal why: for boys, guidelines without enforcement produce the highest non-compliance, reversed only when enforcement penalizes over-reliance; for girls, guidelines alone trigger a sustained decline in violations, consistent with norm internalization. These gender-asymmetric compliance patterns imply that effective governance must solve both a preference-formation and a commitment problem, but the binding constraint differs by gender. Guidelines-only policies may harm the very students they intend to help.       

**Closer is not equal:  The proximity penalty in community-based mental health expansion in China** (with Yuzhi Hao, Xu Zhang) Major Revision

**Abstract**
Community-based mental health expansion in developing countries assumes that physical accessibility and social acceptability are separable dimensions of access. This paper shows they are not: when stigmatized services are delivered through community-embedded channels, physical proximity amplifies stigma's deterrent effect on high-risk individuals, generating severity-graded rather than uniform gains. Using China's 2018–2021 Social Psychological Service System Construction Pilot, difference-in-differences estimates reveal that mental health improvements concentrate entirely among low-risk individuals, with high-risk groups showing no detectable benefit. A discrete choice experiment confirms the mechanism: high-risk respondents are more price-sensitive and less responsive to commuting-time reductions, and proximity differentially magnifies stigma's deterrent effect for them. The paper specifies scope conditions for this proximity penalty and derives severity-contingent delivery-design guidance.
